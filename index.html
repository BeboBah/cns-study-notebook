Macro and microstructure of cerebellum

📄 p. 1–5⏱ 30 minMedium

ILOs: position and general features · parts ↔ functional organisation · deep nuclei and their location · fibres in each peduncle · arteries and their origin · microstructure.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The cerebellum is the back part of the hindbrain.
  • It does not start movements.
  • It adjusts muscle tone, coordination and balance so that movement comes out smooth.
  • This lecture is the map: where it sits, its lobes, its hidden nuclei, its three “cables” (peduncles), its arteries and its three cortical layers.
  • If you learn one thing, learn that each lobe is paired with one job and one deep nucleus.
  • Lectures 2 and 3 are built on that pairing.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Where is it?

  • Posterior part of the hindbrain, in the posterior cranial fossa.
  • In front of it: pons and medulla, separated from it by the fourth ventricle.
  • Above it: the tentorium cerebelli, which separates it from the cerebrum.

What is it made of?

  • A deeply folded cortex. The small gyri are called folia.
  • A core of white matter with the deep nuclei embedded inside.
  • Shape: 2 hemispheres joined in the midline by the narrow vermis (superior vermis on top, inferior vermis below).

Surfaces and fissures

  • Superior surface: fissura prima (primary fissure). It is V-shaped and cuts the superior vermis at the junction between the anterior and posterior lobes.
  • Inferior surface: fissura secunda and the posterolateral fissure. It also shows the cerebellar tonsil.

Lobes

  • Three lobes: anterior, posterior, flocculonodular. Each is divided into lobules.
  • The flocculus is a small part connected to the nodule, which is the most anterior part of the inferior vermis.

Three peduncles = three cables to the brainstem

  • Inferior (ICP): arises from the medulla. Mainly input (afferent) to the anterior lobe from the spinal cord, from different body regions.
  • Middle (MCP): the largest. Carries fibres from the pontine nuclei to the posterior lobe, bringing information from the motor cortex. Also commissural fibres from one side of the cerebellum to the other (cerebello-ponto-cerebellar).
  • Superior (SCP): connects the posterior lobe to the midbrain. Mainly output (efferent) from the deep nuclei, especially the dentate.

Cerebellar cortex: 3 layers, from the surface inwards 📄 figure p. 5

1 · Molecular layer superficial. Few small association neurons and many unmyelinated nerve cell processes.
2 · Purkinje cell layer (ganglionic) one single row of large flask-shaped (pyriform) Purkinje cell bodies with large nuclei, parallel to the surface.
3 · Granular layer innermost. Many granule cells (small stellate cells, large nucleus, little cytoplasm) plus Golgi cells (large stellate neurons; their branching axons stay inside the granular layer).
White matter (medulla) centre of each folium. The deep nuclei lie in the white matter.

Sections are easy to recognise because of the foliate, tree-like appearance.

Cerebellar circuits & functions

📄 p. 6–11⏱ 50 minHard

ILOs: functional divisions · role in motor regulation · feedback vs feed-forward · link with basal ganglia and motor cortex · deep nuclei and what each does.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The cerebellum is the coordinator and predictor of movement.
  • The lecture calls it the “brain inside” because it works unconsciously.
  • It receives a copy of what the cortex ordered and a report of what the body actually did.
  • It compares the two and corrects the difference, and with practice it learns to predict errors before they happen.
  • Every sign of cerebellar disease in Lecture 3 is one of these jobs failing.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Three functional divisions

Deep nuclei: the exit door

  • Four paired nuclei. They relay and process information going from the cortex to targets outside the cerebellum. The dentate is the largest.
  • They receive inhibitory input from Purkinje axons and excitatory collaterals from mossy and climbing fibres.
  • Net output is excitatory (glutamate) to the brainstem, reticular formation, red nucleus, spinal cord and motor thalamus.

Body map (homunculus)

  • Trunk in the midline on the vermis; limbs on the hemispheres.
  • Anterior lobe: extremities. Posterior lobe: mirror-image maps of head and extremities.
  • No body map in the cerebro-cerebellum, because its connections are mainly with the cortex.
  • Each hemisphere talks to the contralateral cerebral cortex through the cortico-ponto-cerebello-dentato-thalamo-cortical circuit, a loop that starts and ends in the cerebral cortex.

Inside the cortex: who excites, who inhibits

  • Climbing fibres: from the inferior olive (medulla). One climbing fibre makes an excitatory synapse with one specific Purkinje cell. Major role in motor learning.
  • Mossy fibres: from many centres in brainstem and spinal cord (vestibular, proprioceptive, and cortical via pontine nuclei). They reach Purkinje cells through interneurons, with multiple synapses.
  • Purkinje cells are the only output of the cortex. Their output is always inhibitory (GABA), onto the deep nuclei and the vestibular nucleus.

Why does that work? The deep nuclei fire continuously. Purkinje cells act like a brake on them, so by pressing harder or lighter they shape the rate, range and direction of movement.

Feedback

  • Compares the plan with the sensory (proprioceptive) signals that result from the movement.
  • Needs no learning, only an accurate comparison.
  • Slow. It can only correct errors that have already happened.

Feed-forward

  • Predicts and prevents errors before they happen, so reactions are quick.
  • Relies on learning from previous experience.
  • Also used for objects and people around us, for example not bumping into people on a busy street.

Vestibulo-cerebellum does

  • Axial stability and balance.
  • Eye movements in response to vestibular input, through the vestibulo-ocular reflex.

Cerebro-cerebellum does

  • Plans and initiates voluntary activity by feeding the cortical motor areas. Stores procedural memories (motor learning).
  • Works with premotor cortex and basal ganglia to plan the sequence of movements (force, timing, direction, rate, range) before they occur. The dentate fires before movement.
  • Calculates the ideal time for onset, progression and end of each muscle’s action.
  • Needed for eye–hand coordination.

Spino-cerebellum does

  1. Servo-comparator. It gets a copy of the motor command (“intention”) and reports from peripheral receptors (“performance”), then corrects the error. The interposed nucleus sends corrections (1) back to motor cortex through the thalamus and (2) to the red nucleus → rubrospinal tract → distal limbs, especially hands and fingers. The result is smooth, coordinated action of agonist and antagonist muscles of the distal limbs.
  2. Damping. Every movement is pendular and tends to overshoot because of momentum. After a short delay the Purkinje circuit sends negative feedback that stops the movement on target. It “puts on the brakes”.
  3. Ballistic movements. Typing and eye saccades are too fast for feedback, so the whole movement is pre-planned to go a set distance and stop.

Effect on the stretch reflex and tone

Cerebro-cerebellum facilitates the stretch reflex (raises tone). Spino-cerebellum inhibits it. Facilitation predominates, so cerebellar disease usually gives hypotonia.

Non-motor functions

Through connections with prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex: working memory, learning, language, visuospatial abilities, emotional functions.

This is the lecture’s own example of what happens when the cerebellum is destroyed. The full list of signs is in Lecture 3.

⭐ MUST KNOW BEFORE THE EXAM

  • Archi = vestibulo = flocculonodular = balance and eyes. Paleo = spino = rate, force, range, direction. Neo = cerebro = planning and initiation.
  • Purkinje cell = only output of the cortex, always inhibitory, GABA. Deep nuclei output = excitatory, glutamate.
  • Climbing fibres from the inferior olive, 1 fibre : 1 Purkinje cell, motor learning. Mossy fibres = everything else.
  • MCP: afferent only. All efferents leave via SCP and ICP.
  • Anterior spinocerebellar tract enters by the SCP and carries the efferent copy. Posterior spinocerebellar and cuneocerebellar enter by the ICP.
  • Feedback is slow and corrects; feed-forward is fast, learned and predicts.
  • Dentate fires before movement. Interposed nucleus corrects distal limb movement via red nucleus and rubrospinal tract.
  • Loss of damping → intention tremor. Cerebellar disease → hypotonia.

  • Picture the deep nuclei as an engine that is always running and the Purkinje cells as the brake pedal. The cerebellum steers by braking. ✏️ tutor note

Cerebellar diseases

📄 p. 12–14⏱ 30 minMedium

ILOs: physiological basis of the symptoms of cerebellar disorders · link each disorder to a normal cerebellar function.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Cerebellar damage does not paralyse and does not cause true muscle weakness.
  • The person can still start and stop movements.
  • What is lost is precision and coordination.
  • This is called ataxia.
  • Each sign is simply one normal function from Lecture 2 that has gone missing: no comparator, no timing, no damping, no braking.
  • Signs appear on the same side as the lesion.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

General rules from the lecture

  • Lesions usually occur in the neocerebellum (neocerebellar syndrome). The lecture states that no lesions occur in the paleocerebellum. ⚑ flag reproduced as written.
  • Lesions of the archicerebellum cause disequilibrium.
  • Lesion of the cortex is temporary; lesion of the deep nuclei is permanent.
  • Deficits are ipsilateral to the lesion.

Causes

  • Stroke
  • Tumours: primary (for example medulloblastoma) or secondary
  • Trauma
  • Degeneration
  • Toxicity and metabolic: alcoholism, anticonvulsant drugs, hypothyroidism
  • Genetic: seven genes linked to spinocerebellar ataxias

Histology of the peripheral nerve, peripheral nerve injury & regeneration

📄 p. 15–18⏱ 30 minMedium

ILOs: the nerve trunk as an organised bundle of axons · structural changes in degeneration and regeneration · the role of the Schwann cell in regeneration.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • A peripheral nerve is a cable.
  • Each axon is a wire, and three connective-tissue coats wrap the wires into bundles and the bundles into a trunk.
  • When the cable is cut, the neuron tries to repair itself in a set of steps called the axon reaction.
  • The Schwann cell is the key helper: it cleans up the debris and then builds a tube that guides the new axon back to its target.
  • Repair only works if the cut ends stay close together.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

🩵 Visual: the three coats, from outside in 📄 figure p. 16

Epineurium · outermost. Dense, irregular, collagenous connective tissue. Its aligned collagen fibres stop the bundle from being over-stretched (resists shearing and traction).
Perineurium · middle. Dense connective tissue, thinner than epineurium. Inner surface lined by several layers of epithelial-like fibroblasts (perineurial cells) with a basal lamina, joined by tight junctions. Organises fibres into bundles (fascicles) and forms the blood–nerve barrier.
Endoneurium · innermost, around each single nerve fibre. Thin layer of reticular fibres (made by the underlying Schwann cells), scattered fibroblasts, fixed macrophages, capillaries, perivascular mast cells. Touches the basal lamina of the Schwann cells.
Axon with its Schwann cell sheath

“HOW?” (the lecture asks): the barrier comes from the tight junctions between perineurial cells, which isolate the neural micro-environment. Diabetes mellitus and some demyelinating disorders disturb it.

Types of nerve fibre

  • By function: sensory (afferent) and motor (efferent).
  • Myelinated: has a neurilemma (Schwann cell cytoplasm and membrane) plus a myelin sheath.
  • Unmyelinated with neurilemmal sheath: Schwann cell sheath only.
  • Naked: no sheath at all. These are the terminal parts of the axon near the synapse.
  • Any bundle, whatever its size, holds sensory and motor, myelinated and unmyelinated axons.

Myelin and the Schwann cell

  • Myelin is a lipid-rich coat made by the Schwann cell, which wraps its membrane around the axon about 50 turns.
  • The sheath is interrupted at the nodes of Ranvier.
  • The stretch between two nodes is an internode = one Schwann cell.
  • The Schwann cell is the neuroglial supporting cell of the peripheral nervous system. It makes myelin and is essential for degeneration and regeneration.

🩵 Visual: the axon reaction in three places 📄 figure p. 18

1 · Local (at the cut)

  • Cut ends retract away from each other.
  • The membrane of each stump fuses over the open end, so axoplasm is not lost.
  • Each end swells as axoplasmic flow piles up.
  • Macrophages and fibroblasts move in. Macrophages and Schwann cells eat the debris.

2 · Anterograde (distal stump)

Wallerian degeneration:

  • Axon terminal swells and degenerates within a week. Contact with the postsynaptic membrane ends.
  • Axon and myelin disintegrate. Schwann cells dedifferentiate and stop making myelin.

Then regeneration:

  • Schwann cells proliferate and form columns, the Schwann tubes, enclosed by basal lamina and endoneurium. Essential for success.

3 · Retrograde (cell body)

Chromatolysis:

  • Perikaryon swollen and spherical. Nissl bodies disperse. Nucleus pushed to the periphery.
  • Starts 2 weeks after injury; may last several months. The cell keeps making ribosomes and proteins.

Then regeneration:

  • Starts at 3 weeks, continues for about 3–6 months.

How the new axon finds its way

Several sprouts leave the proximal stumpEnter the endoneurium and Schwann tubeTube guides the sprout to the targetSchwann cells redifferentiate and myelinate (or form a sheath)First sprout to arrive makes the synapse; the others degenerate

Speed of regeneration: about 3 to 4 mm per day.

  • Use a range of scents (cinnamon, cloves, vanilla, tobacco, mint). Test each nostril while the other is occluded.

  • Symmetry of facial movement is the key.
  • Patient may be unable to whistle or close the eye. Ask them to smile or show the upper teeth, or blow out the cheeks.

Types of facial nerve injury 📄 table and figure p. 26

Supranuclear palsy (upper motor neuron)

  • Lesion above the facial motor nucleus.
  • Affects the lower part of the face only.
  • Forehead still wrinkles; eye still closes.

Infranuclear palsy (lower motor neuron)

  • Lesion distal to the nucleus.
  • Affects both upper and lower parts of the face.
  • No forehead or brow movement; cannot close the eye.

Microstructure of the hearing organ and the vestibular apparatus

📄 p. 36–41⏱ 35 minMedium

ILOs: structure of cochlea and vestibular apparatus · bony vs membranous labyrinth · how each specialised neuroepithelium fits its function · cell types of the macula and organ of Corti.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The membranous labyrinth is mostly plain lining epithelium.
  • In three kinds of place it becomes a specialised neuroepithelium full of hair cells: the organ of Corti (hearing), the macula (linear motion) and the crista ampullaris (rotation).
  • All three work the same way: fluid moves, a jelly-like cover moves, the hairs bend and the cell fires.
  • What differs is the jelly on top and what kind of movement bends it.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

The labyrinths

  • Bony labyrinth: an irregular bony cavity in the petrous temporal bone.
  • Membranous labyrinth: connected ducts and sacs hanging inside it. Lined by epithelium ranging from simple squamous to simple cuboidal.
  • Space between them: perilymphatic space, with perilymph.
  • Endolymph circulates inside the membranous labyrinth. It is close to cytoplasm in composition: rich in K⁺. Its movement activates the receptors.

The vestibular system

📄 p. 42–48⏱ 55 minHard

ILOs: functional roles of the vestibular apparatus · how it detects angular and linear acceleration · the vestibulo-ocular reflex and nystagmus · clinical signs of vestibular dysfunction.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The vestibular system tells the brain where the head is and how it is moving.
  • With that information it does three things: gives you a conscious sense of head position, moves the eyes to keep the image steady, and adjusts trunk and limb muscles so you do not fall.
  • To do this it connects to the spinal cord (vestibulospinal tracts), the cerebellum (flocculonodular lobe, fastigial nucleus) and the eye-muscle nuclei (III, IV, VI).
  • The whole lecture rests on one rule: hairs bent towards the kinocilium excite the cell; bent away, they inhibit it.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Five sense organs on each side

  • Utricle and saccule: receptor = macula.
  • An ampulla at the base of each of the 3 semicircular canals: receptor = crista.
  • Supplied by peripheral axons of vestibular ganglion cells. Central axons run in VIII to the vestibular nuclei. Some bypass the nuclei and end directly in the cerebellum.
  • They monitor two things: angular acceleration and linear acceleration.

The one rule of hair cells

Towards the kinocilium
Depolarised = stimulated. Fires along the vestibular nerve.

Away from the kinocilium
Hyperpolarised = inhibited. Stops firing.

Semicircular canals: rotation

  • Three canals (lateral or horizontal, superior, posterior) at right angles.
  • Detect angular acceleration and deceleration. The cilia bend because the cupula is deflected.
  • Blind to constant velocity. They only detect change: start, stop, change of speed or direction.
  • Each canal works in one plane. Turning head left or right: lateral. Head flexion: superior. Head extension: the inferior (posterior) ampulla.
  • Push–pull: when one side is excited the other is inhibited. The canal towards the direction of rotation is the excited one.

🩵 Visual: the revolving chair, rotating left → right

① Rotation starts

Endolymph lags behind (inertia) and flows opposite to the head. Cupulae of both horizontal canals deflect to the left.

Right crista stimulated, left inhibited → sensation of rotating to the right.

② Constant speed

Endolymph catches up with the head. Cupulae return to rest.

The sense of rotation disappears.

③ Rotation stops

Endolymph keeps moving to the right (momentum). Cupulae deflect to the right.

Left crista stimulated, right inhibited → false feeling of counter-rotation to the left = vertigo. Lasts about 20 seconds, until the cupulae spring back by their elasticity.

Utricle and saccule: linear acceleration and gravity

  • Hair cells are polarised around the striola. Utricle: deflection towards the striola excites. Saccule: deflection away from the striola excites. So each macula covers two dimensions.
  • Utricle lies horizontally: horizontal plane. Saccule is vertical: sagittal plane (up–down, forwards–back).
  • The maculae of the two sides are functionally antagonistic.

What the utricle and saccule do

  • Keep the head vertical with respect to gravity and start many postural reflexes.
  • Head tilt (head-righting reflex): gravity pulls the otoliths, the cilia bend. Only some cells fire at each position, so the pattern tells the direction of tilt. Utricle: forward and backward tilt. Saccule: tilt to either side.
  • Linear acceleration: utricle = horizontal (a bus changing speed). Saccule = vertical (a lift, a jump).
  • Forward acceleration: otoliths of the utricle slide backward → feeling of falling backward → reflex leaning forward.

Central pathways

Primary afferents end mainly in the vestibular nuclei. A few go straight to the vestibulo-cerebellum (flocculonodular lobe and uvula). There are four sub-nuclei: superior, inferior, lateral, medial.

The journey of sounds: “The physiology of hearing”

📄 p. 50–54⏱ 45 minHard

ILOs: auditory receptors (organ of Corti), structure and function · transmission of sound in the outer and middle ear · sound transduction in the inner ear · organisation of the central auditory pathway.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Hearing is the neural perception of sound energy.
  • The ear is a transducer: it changes sound energy into nerve impulses, and the auditory cortex turns those impulses into the sensation of sound.
  • The problem the ear must solve is that sound arrives in air but the receptors sit in fluid, and fluid is much harder to move.
  • The middle ear solves this by boosting the pressure about 22 times.
  • The cochlea then sorts sound by pitch along its length and its hair cells convert movement into electricity.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

What is sound?

A vibrating source sets air molecules vibrating. The waves are longitudinal: alternating compression and decompression (rarefaction) of air molecules that strike the tympanic membrane. Fluid needs greater pressure to move than air because of its greater inertia.

I · External ear

  • Pinna: collects sound over a large area and concentrates it into the small meatus, so more energy reaches the tympanic membrane.
  • External auditory meatus: a tube closed at one end. Up to 30% of sound energy is reflected back: resonance. This adds 15–20 dB at the tympanic membrane over 2–7 kHz.
  • Helps sound localisation. Protects against trauma and foreign material by its tortuous shape, hair and wax.
  • Tympanic membrane: surface area 55 mm². The handle of the malleus is constantly pulled inward by tensor tympani, which keeps the membrane tense, so vibration on any part reaches the malleus. It acts as a resonator and stops vibrating the moment the sound stops: the damping effect.

II · Middle ear

  • Ossicles: the malleus is bound to the incus by a minute ligament, so they move together as a single lever. The incus articulates with the stapes, whose footplate sits in the oval window (loose annular ligament). Each inward movement of the tympanic membrane pushes the stapes into the oval window and makes waves in the inner-ear fluid at the same frequency.
  • Eustachian tube: connects the middle ear with the pharynx. Normally closed at the pharyngeal end; opens on swallowing, chewing, yawning, sneezing. It equalises air pressure on both sides of the tympanic membrane and drains fluid in middle ear inflammation.

  • Basilar membrane: about 25,000 stiff, elastic fibres.

  • Once you know which muscles are still working, you can predict where a paralysed eye will point.

The layers of eyeball: histology of the retina

📄 p. 71–75⏱ 35 minMedium

ILOs: histology of the layers of the eyeball · the layers of the retina · which neurons sit in which layer · where the synapses are.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • This is the microscope view of Lecture 11.
  • Two lists carry the lecture: the 5 layers of the cornea and the 10 layers of the retina.
  • For the retina there is a simple logic.
  • “Nuclear” layers contain cell bodies.
  • “Plexiform” layers contain synapses.
  • Light enters from the vitreous side, but the list is counted from the outside (choroid side) inwards.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

🩵 Visual: cornea, 5 layers front → back 📄 figure p. 71

1 · Corneal epithelium Stratified squamous non-keratinised, 5–7 cell layers. Many free nerve endings (nociceptive): irritation triggers blinking and tears. Mitotic figures present.
2 · Bowman’s membrane Type I collagen. Barrier to the spread of infection. Does not regenerate.
3 · Stroma Thickest layer, about 90%. Collagen fibres are parallel within each lamella but change direction between lamellae. This is what makes the cornea transparent.
4 · Descemet’s membrane Thick basement membrane of the endothelium.
5 · Corneal endothelium Simple squamous epithelium on the inner surface. Metabolic exchange between aqueous humor and cornea.

Rest of the fibrous and vascular coats

  • Sclera: opaque posterior 5/6. Flat collagen bundles running in various directions, plus elastic fibres.
  • Choroid: highly vascular and pigmented, next to the sclera. Supports the retina, absorbs excess light, gives the retina blood and nutrients.
  • Ciliary body: anterior expansion of the choroid; a ring at the limbus. Ciliary muscles: 3 sets of smooth muscle that change lens shape for focusing. Ciliary process: produces aqueous humour.
  • Iris: the most anterior extension of the choroid, between posterior and anterior chambers. Covers the lens except at the pupil.

Lens and vitreous

  • Lens: transparent, biconvex, flexible disc behind the pupil. Concentric layers of lens fibres containing transparent proteins called crystallins. A flexible capsule attaches to the ciliary body by suspensory ligaments. Fine control of focus; the image is reversed and upside down.
  • Vitreous body: transparent, refractile gel of fine collagen fibres and a lot of water. Maintains the shape of the eyeball. Contains phagocytic cells that clear debris.

Retina, part a: pigmented epithelium

  • Outer, non-photosensitive layer, firmly attached to the choroid.
  • A single layer of cuboidal cells. Base: many invaginations. Apex: microvilli that surround the tips of the photoreceptors.
  • Zonula occludens on the lateral membranes forms the blood–retina barrier.

Functions:

  1. Absorbs light after it has stimulated the photoreceptors, preventing reflections that would blur focus.
  2. Phagocytoses spent membranous discs from the tips of the rods.
  3. Esterifies vitamin A derivatives in its smooth endoplasmic reticulum.

Retina, part b: neural layer

  • Photoreceptors: thin, elongated modified neurons lying parallel.
    • Rods: thin, cylindrical outer segments made of flattened discs. More numerous and more sensitive to light.
    • Cones: short, conical outer segments with multiple invaginations.
  • Neurons that process the signal before the optic nerve: ganglion, amacrine, bipolar, horizontal cells.
  • Müller cells: structural and metabolic support.

Refractive media of the eye and visual reflexes

📄 p. 76–88⏱ 50 minHard

ILOs: physics of light · the eye’s refractive media · how the eye focuses light · pupillary light reflex and its clinical value · accommodation reflex · defects in focusing.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • To see clearly, light must be bent so that it lands exactly on the retina.
  • Four transparent structures do the bending: cornea, aqueous humor, lens, vitreous humor.
  • The cornea does most of the work, and the lens does the fine tuning for near objects (accommodation).
  • When the focus falls in front of or behind the retina you get an error of refraction.
  • The lecture ends with two reflexes that doctors test every day: the light reflex and the near (accommodation) reflex.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Physics you need

  • Light = photons, packets of energy with no mass or charge, travelling at 300,000 km/s.
  • Visible spectrum: 400–750 nm. A uniform mix of wavelengths looks white.
  • Refraction: bending of a light ray because its speed changes when it passes from one transparent medium to another, meeting the surface at an oblique angle.
  • Refractive index = velocity of light in air ÷ velocity in the medium. Glass: 300,000 ÷ 200,000 = 1.5.
  • The greater the difference in refractive index and the greater the incident angle, the more the refraction.
  • Focal length: distance between the focal point and the centre of the lens. Power (dioptres) = 1 ÷ focal length (metres). Sharply curved lens = short focal length = stronger.
  • Images are inverted and reversed. The brain is trained to treat the inverted image as normal.

The four refractive media

MediumKey facts and functions
CorneaAbout 2/3 of the dioptric power (≈ 40 D): high refractive index, great curvature, transparent. The most important refractive part. Also protects: hard, absorbs ultraviolet, has receptors for the corneal reflex.
Aqueous humorTransparent, colourless, alkaline fluid in the anterior and posterior chambers. Feeds the avascular cornea and lens. Controls intra-ocular volume and pressure, which stops the eyeball collapsing.
LensAvascular, transparent, biconvex, in an elastic capsule attached to the suspensory ligaments. 1/3 of the refractive power (20 D). Absorbs ultraviolet to protect the retina. Makes near vision possible by accommodation.
Vitreous humorClear gel between lens and retina. Supports the retina and stops the lens moving backward. A weak refractive medium. Keeps the eyeball spherical. Phagocytic cells remove debris.

The visual processing in the retina: visual pathway

📄 p. 89–98⏱ 60 minHard

ILOs: phototransduction · duplicity theory · visual pathways · how visual field deficits point to the site of a lesion · organisation of the visual cortex · how the brain processes vision, including colour.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Photoreceptors are unusual.
  • In the dark they are switched on (depolarised) and release glutamate all the time.
  • Light switches them off.
  • That is the surprise at the heart of this lecture, and once you accept it the cascade is easy.
  • The signal then travels: photoreceptor → bipolar cell → ganglion cell → optic nerve → chiasma → tract → lateral geniculate body → visual cortex.
  • Rods and cones split the work: cones for day, detail and colour; rods for night.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

The retina in 4 functional layers (outside → inside)

1 · Pigmented layer Touches the choroid. Black melanin stops light reflecting inside the eye. Stores large amounts of vitamin A. Feeds the photoreceptors.
2 · Rods and cones Outer segment: photosensitive compounds. Inner segment: nucleus, many mitochondria. Synaptic zone.
3 · Bipolar cells First-order neuron of the visual pathway.
4 · Ganglion cells Their axons pierce choroid and sclera at the blind spot and form the optic nerve.

The retina lines the posterior 2/3 of the eyeball. Light has to cross the ganglion and bipolar cells first to reach the receptors on the outer side.

Special areas of the retina

  • Optic disc (blind spot): 3 mm medial to and slightly above the posterior pole. Nerve fibres only, so no vision.
  • Macula lutea: small yellow area near the posterior pole. Few rods, mostly cones.
  • Fovea centralis: oval depression in the centre of the macula. Cones only.
  • Extreme periphery: rods only. In between, both.

Why is the fovea so sharp?

  1. The inner layers are pulled aside, so light hits the receptors directly. No blood vessels.
  2. Foveal cones are very thin and densely packed. 1 cone → 1 bipolar → 1 ganglion cell: a private line to the cortex. This gives acute vision, and a high light threshold.

Elsewhere many rods share one bipolar cell, and groups of bipolar cells share one ganglion cell. This convergence lets signals summate, so rods are sensitive but not sharp.

🩵 Visual: phototransduction, dark versus light 📄 figures p. 92–93

🌙 In the dark: “dark current”

  • cGMP is high, so cGMP-gated Na⁺ channels in the outer segment stay open. Na⁺ leaks in.
  • The inner segment pumps Na⁺ out and K⁺ in (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase); K⁺ leaks out through non-gated channels.
  • Membrane potential about −40 mV instead of the usual −70 to −80 mV. The rod is depolarised.
  • Glutamate is released continuously.

☀️ In the light

  1. Rhodopsin absorbs light: cis-retinal → all-trans.
  2. Metarhodopsin II (activated rhodopsin) forms.
  3. It activates the G-protein transducin, which activates cGMP phosphodiesterase: cGMP → 5′-GMP.
  4. cGMP falls, Na⁺ channels close, inward Na⁺ current drops. The rod hyperpolarises towards −70 to −80 mV. The brighter the light, the greater the hyperpolarisation (graded).
  5. Glutamate release is inhibited.

Switching off after the flash

  • Rhodopsin kinase inactivates metarhodopsin II.
  • Light lowers Ca²⁺ (as well as Na⁺) in the photoreceptor. Low Ca²⁺ activates guanylyl cyclase, which remakes cGMP, and inhibits the phosphodiesterase.

Next cells in line

  • Bipolar cells: first step of data processing. Input from a group of rods or a single cone. Their glutamate receptor is coupled through an inhibitory G-protein to a cation channel, so glutamate (dark) keeps the channel shut. In light, glutamate falls, the channel opens and the bipolar cell depolarises in a graded way: excited when light is turned ON.
  • Ganglion cells: generate action potentials that carry the information to the brain.

Anterior and posterior segments of the eye and treatment of glaucoma

📄 p. 99–104⏱ 40 minMedium

ILO (stated in part 2): explain the mechanisms of action of the drugs used for chronic open-angle glaucoma. The first part covers intraocular fluid, intraocular pressure and glaucoma.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The eye is kept firm by fluid.
  • Aqueous humor in front of the lens flows freely and is constantly made and drained.
  • Vitreous humor behind the lens is a gel that hardly flows.
  • Intraocular pressure depends on the balance between making and draining aqueous.
  • In glaucoma the drain is blocked, pressure rises and the optic nerve is crushed.
  • Every glaucoma drug does one of two things: makes less aqueous or drains more.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Vitreous humor (vitreous body)

  • Gelatinous mass held by a fine fibrillar network of very long proteoglycan molecules. Water and solutes diffuse slowly; there is little flow.
  • Functions: keeps the eye round · helps clarity of vision · absorbs shocks, such as a thump to the side of the head.

How aqueous humor is made

  • Rate: 2 to 3 microlitres per minute. Almost all by the ciliary processes (folds of the ciliary body covered by secretory epithelium over a highly vascular area).
  • It is an active secretion.
Active transport of Na⁺Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ follow (electrical neutrality)Water follows by osmosis from the capillaries

Amino acids, ascorbic acid and glucose are also carried across, by active transport or facilitated diffusion.

  • Conventional (trabecular), 60–80%: trabecular meshwork → canal of Schlemm → aqueous veins → episcleral venous system.

Glaucoma

Increased resistance to outflow through the trabecular spaces into the canal of Schlemm (iridocorneal junction)Intraocular pressure above 25–30 mm HgCompression of optic nerve fibres and the retinal artery at the optic discPoor nutrition of the retina, neuronal damageBlindness, within days or even hours if pressure is extreme

Open-angle (chronic)

  • The most common form: at least 90% of cases.
  • The lecture’s explanation: large amounts of debris in the aqueous (after haemorrhage into the eye or intraocular infection) collect in the trabecular spaces and prevent adequate reabsorption.

Closed-angle (acute, narrow-angle)

  • Less common. Develops very quickly.
  • The iris bulges and partly or completely blocks the drainage angle.

Treatment

Acute / narrow-angle: two goals

  1. Pull the iris away from the sclera. Constrict the pupil and contract the ciliary body to widen the trabecular meshwork: pilocarpine, a parasympathomimetic.
  2. Drop the pressure fast. Mannitol, an osmotic diuretic, draws fluid out of the eye by osmosis. Acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase → no bicarbonate → less Na⁺ transport → less aqueous.

Chronic / open-angle: two goals

  1. Lower formation: timolol, brimonidine or acetazolamide.
  2. Increase drainage: latanoprost or brimonidine.

Timolol, latanoprost and brimonidine all have the merit of not affecting accommodation or pupil size.

Smell and taste

📄 p. 111–117⏱ 35 minMedium

ILOs: neural elements of the olfactory epithelium · signal transduction in odorant receptors · location and cells of taste buds · the five taste receptors and their transduction · the pathway from taste receptors to the cortex.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Smell and taste are the chemical senses.
  • They let us separate unpleasant from pleasant food and they trigger digestive responses.
  • They are built differently, and examiners like that contrast.
  • The olfactory receptor is a real neuron (a bipolar cell whose own axon goes to the brain).
  • The taste receptor is not a neuron; it is a cell that excites a nerve ending.
  • Smell goes straight to the limbic system, which is why odours stir emotion and memory.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Olfactory epithelium

In the superior part of the nasal cavity: covers the inferior surface of the cribriform plate and extends along the superior nasal concha.

CellWhat it is and does
Olfactory receptor cellBipolar neuron, the first-order neuron. Knob-shaped dendrite carrying several non-motile cilia (the site of transduction). Its axon passes through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb.
Supporting (sustentacular) cellColumnar epithelial cell with microvilli and secretory granules.
Basal cellUndifferentiated stem cell at the base. Gives rise to new receptor cells.

Bowman’s glands in the supporting connective tissue make mucus, which moistens the surface and dissolves odorants so transduction can occur.

How we tell smells apart, and rules for odorants

  • There are many types of olfactory receptor. Each reacts to only a select group of odorants.
  • Genetic studies suggest hundreds of primary odours. We recognise about 10,000 odours from the pattern of activity across many combinations of receptor cells.
  • An odorant must be volatile (so it can be sniffed in) and at least slightly water-soluble (so it can cross the mucus to the cilia).
  • Low threshold: a few molecules are enough. The cascade multiplies the effect of even the weakest odorant by opening very large numbers of channels.

Adaptation

  • Receptors adapt about 50% in the first second, then very little and very slowly.
  • Most adaptation is central: centrifugal fibres run back along the olfactory tract and end on inhibitory granule cells in the olfactory bulb. The same seems true for taste.

Medial olfactory area

The primitive system. Nuclei in the mid-basal brain just anterior to the hypothalamus. Feeds the hypothalamus and primitive limbic system.

Carries the most fibres. The primary olfactory cortex: prepyriform and pyriform cortex plus the cortical part of the amygdaloid nuclei.

Projects to almost all of the limbic system, especially the hippocampus: learning to like or dislike foods, the emotional character of odours, recalling memories.

Newer pathway

Through the dorsomedial nucleus of the thalamus to the orbitofrontal cortex.

Local anaesthetics

📄 p. 126–128⏱ 30 minMedium

ILOs: techniques of giving local anaesthetics · mechanism of action and adverse reactions · factors affecting neuronal blockade.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Local anaesthetics are agents that abolish sensation (and, at higher concentration, motor activity) without loss of consciousness, in a limited area of the body.
  • They work by plugging the sodium channel of the nerve axon so no action potential can pass.
  • Three properties of the drug decide how it behaves: lipid solubility = potency, protein binding = duration, and ionisation = how much can get in.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

🩵 Visual: esters versus amides 📄 table p. 126

The molecule is a lipophilic group joined by an ester or amide link to a hydrophilic part. The linking chain decides the class.

Mechanism of action 📄 figure p. 127

Reversible block of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels of neuronal axons, from the intracellular side.

Outside the nerve: only the lipid-soluble, un-ionised drug can cross the membrane
Inside the axon: the drug reaches the channel from the cytoplasm
It binds protein within the Na⁺ channel and blocks it
No Na⁺ entry → no action potential → no sensation

Factors related to the drug

  • Lipid solubility → potency. Better diffusion through the membranes of the axons. Bupivacaine is more lipid-soluble than lidocaine, so more potent.
  • Ionisation constant (pKa) against tissue pH sets how much drug is un-ionised (lipid-soluble) and how much is ionised (water-soluble). In infected tissue, pus lowers the pH and changes the degree of ionisation.
  • Protein binding → duration. High affinity for protein within the sodium channel gives a sustained block. Bupivacaine has the greatest protein binding and is the longest acting.
  • A vasoconstrictor, e.g. adrenaline: reduces absorption, so the local concentration rises and the action lasts longer. Less systemic absorption also means less systemic toxicity.
  • Dose.

🩵 Visual: which fibres are blocked first? 📄 figures p. 128

Fibre diameter

Small fibres first. Type A-delta and C fibres (pain) are blocked before larger fibres (touch and motor).

Myelination

For the same diameter, myelinated nerves are blocked before non-myelinated. The non-myelinated C fibres (pain) still have a high sensitivity to block.

Use-dependent block

Nerves that fire faster are more sensitive, because the drug binds best when Na⁺ channels are open. Channels in the open and inactivated states are the most sensitive. Pain fibres fire faster than motor fibres.

Painblocked first, at low concentrationTouchMotorblocked last, at higher concentration

This is why the definition says sensation is lost first and motor activity only at higher concentration.

🏥 Clinical Corner — Understanding What Goes Wrong

Systemic adverse reactions

Cause: accidental injection into the systemic circulation, or rapid absorption from the site (for example a highly vascular area). Toxicity shows in organs that depend on sodium channels: the central nervous system and the heart.

CNS toxicity (early)

  • Dizziness, tinnitus
  • Generalised convulsions
  • Circumoral paraesthesia: a common early neurotoxic sign

Cardiotoxicity

Bupivacaine can cause negative chronotropic and conduction effects, raising the risk of ventricular arrhythmias, including ventricular fibrillation.

Anaphylaxis

More likely with ester local anaesthetics, with cross-allergy to other ester-containing compounds.

Precautions: use the smallest dose, a proper injection technique and a local vasoconstrictor.

Local adverse reactions

Irritation and inflammation at the site of injection.

Antidepressants

📄 p. 144–147⏱ 50 minHard

ILOs: mechanism of action of each antidepressant · indications and contraindications · other uses · choosing the right drug for the clinical setting.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Antidepressants are drugs meant to control major depression.
  • Depression is linked to a deficiency of monoamines, and these drugs push the levels back towards normal.
  • They do it in one of two ways: they block reuptake at the synapse, or they block breakdown by monoamine oxidase.
  • All of them need 2 to 4 weeks to work.
  • Learn each class by what it blocks, because its side effects follow from that.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Two mechanisms

  • Block reuptake. They block the transporters for serotonin (SERT), norepinephrine (NET) or dopamine (DAT). Site of action: the synaptic cleft.
  • Block breakdown. They inhibit monoamine oxidase in the nerve ending. Site of action: the presynaptic terminal.

Why the 2–4 week delay?

Drug raises serotonin at once
Inhibitory autoreceptors (5-HT1A on the cell body, 5-HT1B on the axon) respond by cutting serotonin release
Over weeks the autoreceptors desensitise (downregulate)
Release rises and mood improves

Rules of treatment

  • Continue for at least 6 months at full dose, to reduce relapse.
  • Still symptomatic? Combine antidepressants or add an augmenter (anxiolytic or mood stabiliser).
  • Withdraw very gradually, and taper the first drug when switching. Otherwise: withdrawal symptoms, rebound agitation, relapse.
  • Suicide risk may rise in the early weeks, especially under 25, because energy returns before mood does. Monitor closely (weekly at first); admit high-risk patients.
  • Usual start: one drug, often an SSRI. A short course of a benzodiazepine can cover severe anxiety, agitation or insomnia.

  • Anxiety is an unpleasant emotional experience: fear out of proportion to the stress, or fear without a cause.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Drugs used in anxiety

  • Anxiolytics: benzodiazepines (they replaced the barbiturates) and buspirone.
  • Some antidepressants: SSRIs such as sertraline, SNRIs such as venlafaxine.
  • Beta-blockers such as propranolol do not treat the psychological part. They calm the body signs: tremor, sweating, palpitations. Used off-label for performance anxiety (“stage fright”).

How benzodiazepines work

Bind their own site on the GABA-A receptor, between its α and γ subunits
Positive allosteric modulators: they help GABA, they do not replace it
The chloride channel opens more often
Cl⁻ enters, the neuron hyperpolarises and fires less

Effects: anxiolytic, sedative, hypnotic, muscle relaxant, anticonvulsant.

Used alone in acute anxiety; with an antidepressant, only short-term, in chronic anxiety.

Meninges & CSF formation

📄 p. 151–156⏱ 25 minEasy

ILOs: the meningeal layers around brain and spinal cord · the clinical importance of the spaces between them · formation and circulation of CSF · related clinical conditions.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The meninges are three membranes that wrap the brain and spinal cord, with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) between them.
  • Together they cushion the brain and let it float, which reduces its effective weight so it is not squeezed by its own mass.
  • The spaces between the layers matter clinically: that is where blood collects, where CSF flows and where the needle goes in a lumbar puncture.
  • CSF is made by the choroid plexuses and returned to the venous blood through the arachnoid villi.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Increased intracranial pressure and hydrocephalus

📄 p. 165–168⏱ 45 minHard

ILOs: pathophysiology of raised intracranial pressure · communicating and non-communicating hydrocephalus · head injury, traumatic intracranial haemorrhage and brain oedema · outcomes of raised pressure and brain herniation.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • The skull is a closed box.
  • Any increase in the volume of what is inside raises the intracranial pressure.
  • The causes are hydrocephalus, haemorrhage, generalised cerebral oedema, ischaemia and mass lesions such as tumours.
  • High pressure reduces blood flow to the brain, which causes more swelling, and in the end pushes brain tissue from one compartment into another.
  • That last step is herniation, and it kills.
  • The lecture also covers head injury, where the key skill is telling an epidural from a subdural haematoma.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Hydrocephalus

An increase in the volume of CSF within the ventricular system, from impaired flow or decreased resorption.

MechanismExample
More production, normal outflowCancer of the choroid plexus
Normal production, less outflowA ventricular mass or obstruction
Normal production, less resorptionArachnoid fibrosis

Communicating

The whole ventricular system is enlarged. Usually from reduced CSF resorption.

Non-communicating

Part of the system enlarges, behind a local block. Most often a mass at the foramen of Monro or pressing on the aqueduct.

Outcome: in infancy, before the sutures close, the head enlarges. After the sutures fuse, the ventricles expand and pressure rises with no change in head size. Hydrocephalus ex vacuo is a harmless compensatory rise in CSF after loss of brain volume (infarction, neurodegenerative disease).

Head injury: what gets damaged

  • Scalp: contusions, lacerations. Skull: depressed fractures. Skull-base fractures are missed on X-ray and may leak CSF from the nose or bleed into the middle ear. Orbital fractures bleed into the orbit.
  • Focal brain damage shows on CT or MRI and may be operable: contusions, lacerations, intracerebral haemorrhage.
  • Diffuse brain damage is microscopic: an unconscious patient with an almost normal scan. Diffuse axonal injury, hypoxic–ischaemic damage, diffuse brain swelling.
  • Severe brain damage can occur with no external sign of injury, and a skull fracture does not prove the brain is damaged.

Contusion and laceration

  • Laceration: the brain is penetrated by a projectile or bone fragment.
  • Contusion: a bruise at the site of impact (coup) or on the opposite side (contrecoup). It affects the crests of the gyri, as a wedge of haemorrhagic necrosis widest at the surface. Old lesions are depressed yellowish-brown patches.

📄 p. 169–174⏱ 35 minMedium

ILOs: classify inflammatory lesions by site into meningitis and encephalitis · features of bacterial and viral meningitis · link each infection to its clinical presentation.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Infections of the nervous system are classified by where they sit: around the dura, in the leptomeninges (meningitis) or in the brain substance (encephalitis and abscess).
  • The brain’s own anatomy makes them dangerous.
  • It is bathed in CSF, which is both a culture medium and a fast road for spread, and every vessel of the brain crosses that fluid-filled space.
  • The most examined skill is reading the CSF.
  • Neutrophils with low sugar point to bacteria; lymphocytes with normal sugar point to a virus.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

How infection gets in, and how it injures

  • Blood (arterial or venous).
  • Up the peripheral nerves: rabies and herpes simplex viruses.
  • Direct: penetrating trauma, or iatrogenic (lumbar puncture).
  • Local extension from the sinuses, mastoid or middle ear.

Injury comes from: the organism itself · its toxins · the inflammatory response · immune mechanisms.

Epidural and subdural infections

  • Follow trauma, or infection in the paranasal sinuses or mastoid.
  • Virulent organisms: staphylococci and streptococci.
  • Epidural infection stays localised. Subdural infection may spread widely.

CNS Tumors

📄 p. 175–179⏱ 35 minMedium

ILOs: clinical presentations and demographics of CNS tumours · the WHO grading system and prognosis · primary versus metastatic tumours.

🩷 The Big Picture

  • Brain tumours are either primary (one half to three quarters) or metastatic (the rest).
  • They are named after the cell they come from, and a WHO grade from 1 to 4 predicts how they behave.
  • Two patterns help most: adults get tumours in the cerebral hemispheres, and children get them in the posterior fossa.
  • Even a benign tumour can kill if it sits in a critical place.
  • They present with raised intracranial pressure, focal deficits or seizures.

💜 Detailed but Simplified Explanation

Classification

  • Neuroepithelial tumours. Gliomas: astrocytic tumours (infiltrating: diffuse astrocytoma II, anaplastic astrocytoma III, glioblastoma IV; localised: pilocytic astrocytoma I), oligodendroglioma, ependymoma. Also choroid plexus, neuronal and pineal tumours. Embryonal: medulloblastoma IV.
  • Cranial nerve tumours: schwannoma, neurofibroma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour.
  • Meninges: meningioma.
  • Sarcomas · lymphoma · germ cell tumours.
  • Sellar region: pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma.
  • Metastatic tumours.

General features

  • CNS tumours are up to 20% of all childhood cancers.
  • Adults: 70% arise in the cerebral hemispheres. Children: the posterior fossa.
  • Common in childhood: pilocytic astrocytoma, ependymoma, medulloblastoma.
  • They rarely spread outside the CNS, but can seed through the subarachnoid space.
  • Infiltrating astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas invade widely, so curative removal is impossible.
  • They present with: raised intracranial pressure · focal neurological deficits · seizures.

🩵 Visual: WHO grades

Grade 1 Low proliferative potential. Cure is possible after surgical removal.
Grade 2 Infiltrative, but still slow. May recur and progress to a higher grade.
Grade 3 Malignant under the microscope: nuclear atypia and more mitoses.
Grade 4 Malignant, mitotically active, prone to necrosis. Rapid progression and a fatal outcome.

Infiltrating astrocytomas tend to climb from a low grade to a higher one with time.
